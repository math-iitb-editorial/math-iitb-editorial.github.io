Philosophy of Mathematics

Author: Sandeep Birua
Published: 2026-05-08

Throughout the world, philosophy and mathematics have a unique manner of intertwining with each other. From Vedic philosophy to Buddhist ideology, even among Western philosophers, the appearance of mathematical concepts in philosophical thinking is a common occurrence. Here we will display some elegant instances where philosophy and mathematics collide.

Vedic Philosophy

ॐ पूर्णमदः पूर्णमिदं पूर्णात्पूर्णमुदच्यते
पूर्णस्य पूर्णमादाय पूर्णमेवावशिष्यते ॥
ॐ शांतिः शांतिः शांतिः ॥

That is Whole, this is Whole;
from the Whole, the Whole becomes manifest.
From the Whole, when the Whole is negated, what
remains is again the Whole

From the Isha Upanishad

This verse from the Isha Upanishads is claimed by many scholars to have deep ties to the concept of infinity in mathematics. The Sanskrit word translated as "Whole" is Purna, which literally means complete, full, or infinite. When interpreted mathematically, the verse is essentially a statement about the property of infinity.

In the first line, "That is Whole, this is Whole", "That" represents the unmanifest, Absolute Reality (Brahman) and "This" represents the manifest universe, the phenomenal world (Maya). The verse posits that the Source (That) and the Creation (This) are both fundamentally complete and infinite. The manifest universe isn't a part of the Absolute, but is the Absolute expressing itself.

\[ \infty - (\text{any number}) = \infty \]

The second line, "From the Whole, the Whole becomes manifest", describes the act of creation or emanation. The universe (Whole) is generated from the Source (Whole). This can be interpreted as the mathematical concept of subtracting an infinite quantity (the created universe) from another infinite quantity (the source), with the source remaining unchanged. Though in mathematics infinity minus infinity is undefined, in this philosophical context, it is still deemed infinite. It implies that the universe "comes out of" the Absolute without making the Absolute any less whole.

\[ \infty_S - \infty_M = \infty \]

$\infty_S$ is the original Source (the Whole)    $\infty_M$ is the Manifest World (the Whole that is negated)

The final line, "From the Whole, when the Whole is negated, what remains is again the Whole", describes the act of dissolution or negation (negating the manifest universe). Mathematically, this means that when the manifest universe {Whole} dissolves back into the source (Whole), the source remains exactly as it was: Whole and Infinite.

Together, this verse can be seen as explaining the mathematical property:

\[ \infty \pm \infty = \infty \]

The verse shows that the concept of Purna (Wholeness/Infinity) is indestructible and unquantifiable by finite terms. You can take the Whole away from the Whole, and the remainder is still the Whole, because the Whole is not a finite quantity that can be divided or lessened.

Bhaskara II and the Arithmetic of Infinity

"Bīja Gaṇita" or "The Algebra of the Hindus", a mathematical treatise written by the 12th-century Indian mathematician and astronomer Bhaskara II (also known as Bhaskaracharya), writes:

आस्मन वकारः स्वहरेण राशावप्रवेऽप नःसृतेषु
बहुष्वप स्याल्स्यृकालेऽनन्तेऽच्युते भूतमगणे यद्वत्॥ २०॥

asmaan vikaarah svahareṇa raasaavapravishtepi nihsriteshu
bahushvapi syaalasrishtikaalenaantechyutate bhootamagane yadvat

Just as at the time of delusion all beings enter the endless changeless and at the time of creation emerge from the infinite God and by these acts the infinite remains unaffected in the same way to this quantity with zero divisor if we add or from this if we remove large quantities, there cannot be any change in it.

Mathematically, Bhaskaracharya is describing the properties of Infinity. The verse asserts a fundamental law of transfinite arithmetic:

\[ \textbf{Addition: } \infty + k = \infty \qquad \textbf{Subtraction: } \infty - k = \infty \]

Bhaskara explains that, just as adding or removing finite amounts to the Infinite (God/Universe) does not change its nature, adding or subtracting any quantity from a number divided by zero does not change its value.

Bhaskara uses a cosmological metaphor to make this abstract math understandable to his students:

The Cosmic Cycle: In Indian philosophy, the universe undergoes cycles of Srishti (creation/emergence) and Laya (dissolution/absorption).
The Infinite (Ananta/Achyuta): During creation, all beings emerge from the Infinite God (Brahman); during dissolution, all beings enter back into Him.
The Immutable Nature: Despite the emergence and absorption of countless beings and worlds, the Infinite itself remains "unchanged" and "undiminished."

This text is significant for several reasons:

Definition of Infinity: It shows that Indian mathematicians had a sophisticated understanding of infinity as a mathematical entity that remains constant regardless of finite operations.
Zero as a Number: It reinforces the Indian tradition of treating zero not just as a placeholder, but as a number with specific operational properties.
Integration of Knowledge: It highlights the classical Indian approach of blending science, mathematics, and philosophy, using poetic and spiritual metaphors to teach rigorous logic.

Advait Vedanta and Mathematics

Vedanta, particularly the Advaita (non-dual) school, functions much like a geometric proof.

First Principles: Mathematics starts with axioms (self-evident truths). Vedanta starts with the "Self" (Atman) as the one undeniable axiom; you cannot deny your own existence because the denier must exist to deny.

Negation (Neti Neti): Mathematicians often use "proof by contradiction" (reductio ad absurdum). Similarly, Vedantists use Neti Neti ("not this, not that") to strip away false identities, leaving only the irreducible truth. Mathematics often seeks a Grand Unified Theory or a single equation that explains all physical phenomena.

Brahman as the Substratum: Vedanta posits that while we see a "multiplicity" of things (people, stars, objects), they are all modifications of one underlying substance (Brahman).

Fractal Nature: Similar to how a fractal maintains the same pattern regardless of scale, Vedanta suggests that the "Microcosm" (Atman) is identical in nature to the "Macrocosm" (Brahman). The concept of Maya is often misunderstood as "illusion," but it more accurately refers to a "measurement" or "construct."

The Map vs The Territory: In math, we use models (like a graph of a wave) to represent reality. The model is "real" in the sense of a description, but it isn't the actual physical water.

Relativity: Vedanta argues that our perception of the world is a relative construct. This aligns with mathematical physics, which shows that "solid" matter is actually mostly empty space governed by probability fields—our sensory "math" just interprets it as solid.

Jainism and Mathematics

There is a profound and historical connection between Jainism and mathematics. Unlike many other ancient traditions where mathematics was primarily used for trade or architecture, Jain thinkers viewed Mathematics as an essential religious requirement and a path to spiritual clarity.

Jain texts, such as the Satkhandagama (2nd century CE), contain operations that look remarkably like modern Set Theory. They used "existential sets" and "constructive sets" to explain the number of souls and types of matter in the universe.

Jain philosophy centres on Syadvada (the doctrine of "may-be" or "conditional predication"). This is a seven-fold system of logic called Saptabhangi. The Jains were pioneers in Combinatorics (Vikalpa). This was driven by a religious need to calculate all the possible combinations of different "atoms" (Paramanus) or "karmic particles" that could bind to a soul.

Islam and Mathematics

In Islamic thought, mathematics is not merely a tool for trade or calculation, but a bridge between the physical world and the divine. While the Golden Age of Islam (c. 8th–14th centuries) produced foundational breakthroughs in algebra and trigonometry, these advancements were deeply rooted in Islamic theology and the practical needs of the faith. The core of Islam is Tawhid, the absolute oneness of God. This concept is mirrored in Islamic Sacred Geometry.

Islamic Art: In Islamic art, almost every complex pattern begins with a simple circle. The circle represents primordial unity and the source of all diversity. Intricate geometric patterns (tessellations) represent how the "many" (creation) originate from the "One" (the Creator). Because the patterns can theoretically repeat forever, they symbolise the infinite nature of God.

The word Algebra comes from the Arabic Al-Jabr, meaning "restoration" or "completion." This was coined by the mathematician Al-Khwarizmi. The Quran emphasises that God created the universe in "due measure" and balance. Algebra—the act of balancing two sides of an equation—was seen as a mathematical way to mirror this cosmic justice and equilibrium. Early Muslim mathematicians often wrote that they developed algebra to solve complex problems of inheritance law (Fara'id), ensuring that wealth was distributed exactly as prescribed by divine law.

Islamic scholars like Al-Kindi and Al-Farabi argued that mathematics was a prerequisite for studying philosophy and theology. Just as mathematics moves from undeniable axioms to certain conclusions, Islamic logic sought to use reason to confirm the truths of revelation. Many medieval Muslim thinkers believed that, because mathematics is universal and unchanging, it is the "language" the Creator uses to write the laws of the universe (Kauniyyah).

Buddhism and Mathematics

While Advait Vedanta focuses on the Infinite, Buddhism's relationship with mathematics is centred on the Zero, the nature of change, and logic. Buddhist philosophy, particularly the Madhyamaka school, shares a deep conceptual (and some argue historical) bond with mathematical structures.

The most direct historical link is the word Śūnya. In Buddhist philosophy, Śūnyatā (Emptiness) does not mean "nothingness" in the sense of a void; rather, it means that things are "empty of a permanent, independent essence." When Indian mathematicians developed the decimal system, they used the word Śūnya for zero. Just as a mathematical zero has no value on its own but allows all other numbers to function in a positional system, "Emptiness" in Buddhism is the "background" that allows all phenomena to arise and interact.

In modern set theory, the Empty Set is a set containing nothing. Yet, it is the fundamental building block from which all other numbers can be constructed (e.g., the number 1 can be defined as the set containing the empty set). This mirrors the Buddhist idea that reality is built out of empty "building blocks." Buddhism is famous for the doctrine of Anicca (Impermanence), the idea that nothing is static and everything is a process.

In Calculus, a derivative measures the rate of change at a single, infinitesimal point. Buddhism views life similarly: there is no "fixed" person, only a continuous stream of "moments" (Kshanas) changing so rapidly they create the illusion of a solid object. Just as calculus shifts math from studying static shapes to studying motion, Buddhism shifts philosophy from studying "Beings" to studying "Becomings."

Catuṣkoṭi: Four-Valued Logic
Traditional Western logic (and most basic math) is binary: a statement is either True or False. This is the Law of the Excluded Middle. Buddhist logic, specifically the Catuṣkoṭi, uses a four-valued system for complex metaphysical questions:

True (Affirmation)
False (Negation)
Both True and False
Neither True nor False

This bears striking resemblance to Fuzzy Logic or Quantum Logic, in which a state (such as a qubit) can exist in a superposition of 0 and 1 simultaneously.

West and Mathematics

In Western intellectual history, philosophy and mathematics are not just similar; they were essentially the same discipline for over two thousand years. The "Scientific Revolution" and the modern university system eventually separated them, but their core ideas remain deeply entwined.

The most famous link is Platonic Realism. Plato argued that the physical world is just a shadow of a more "real" world of perfect Forms. Mathematical objects like a "perfect circle" or the "number 7" do not exist in the physical world (you can only find imperfect circles). Most mathematicians today are "Platonists"—they believe they are discovering mathematical truths that already exist in an abstract realm, rather than inventing them.

Logic is the "bridge" between the two. Historically, logic was a branch of philosophy (the study of valid reasoning), but in the 19th century, it became a branch of mathematics. Both fields rely on Axioms (starting assumptions) and Deduction (drawing conclusions that must be true if the axioms are true). Philosophers like Bertrand Russell and Alfred North Whitehead tried to prove in their book Principia Mathematica that all of mathematics could be reduced to pure logic. This effort eventually led to the creation of computer science.

During the Enlightenment, philosophers like René Descartes (who invented the x-y coordinate plane) and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (who co-invented Calculus) believed that the "mathematical method" was the only way to find absolute truth in philosophy.

He wanted to build a philosophy that was as certain as geometry. His famous "I think, therefore I am" was intended as a "philosophical axiom" from which all other knowledge could be derived. He imagined a "universal characteristic"—a mathematical language in which philosophers could stop arguing and instead say, "Let us calculate" to find the answer to any moral or metaphysical problem.

The conceptual heavy lifting for understanding "The Infinite" was done by both theologians/philosophers and mathematicians. Ancient Greek philosophers used mathematical puzzles (like Achilles and the Tortoise) to argue that motion might be an illusion. Immanuel Kant argued that space and time aren't "things" out there in the world, but are the "internal geometry" of the human mind, which is why mathematics fits the world so perfectly.
