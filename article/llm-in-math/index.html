From Hallucinated Arithmetic to Olympiad Problems

Author: Nilabha Saha
Published: 2026-04-02

Large language models (LLMs), the technology behind systems like ChatGPT, Gemini, and Claude, are, at their core, autoregressive next-token predictors: they are trained to predict the next token in a sequence from vast text corpora. There is no built-in notion of number, quantity, or logical entailment in the basic objective. A model that has learnt to write fluent English has not, in any obvious sense, learnt to add. And yet, in roughly four to five years, LLMs went from struggling with basic multi-step arithmetic to solving difficult olympiad problems. In this article, we will trace that arc: the early failures, the benchmarks that defined progress, and some of the techniques that made it happen.

To understand why maths is hard for LLMs, one must understand how LLMs see numbers. A tokeniser, the component that converts raw text into units the model processes, does not treat "1234" as the number "one thousand, two hundred, and thirty-four"; language models process text as token sequences rather than as explicit symbolic numerals with place-value semantics. Consequently, early GPT-era models such as GPT-2 (released in 2019) and GPT-3 (introduced in 2020) were not reliable multi-digit calculators. GPT-3, for example, showed some ability on small arithmetic tasks but still struggled on robust multi-step reasoning.

Progress became measurable thanks to two benchmarks. One of them is GSM8K (Cobbe et al., OpenAI, 2021), which consists of roughly 8,500 grade-school word problems, with solutions typically requiring between two and eight reasoning steps. The other, substantially harder benchmark, is MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021): a dataset of 12,500 competition-style mathematics problems with full step-by-step solutions. When MATH was introduced, the paper emphasised that accuracy remained low even for very large transformer models. These two benchmarks, one testing elementary multi-step reasoning and the other much more advanced mathematical problem solving, became standard yardsticks for subsequent work.

Prompting and Inference-Time Techniques

The first major breakthrough was chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting, introduced by Wei et al. in 2022. The idea is that instead of asking the model to jump straight from question to answer, you prompt it to produce intermediate reasoning steps. The original paper showed that, for sufficiently large models, this can dramatically improve performance on arithmetic and reasoning benchmarks, including GSM8K. The belief is that the model uses its own generated text as a kind of external working memory, turning one hard inference into a sequence of easier ones. Effectively, the model is being pushed to produce something closer to a proof trace rather than merely a final claim.

A natural extension is self-consistency decoding (Wang et al., 2022): instead of taking one greedy reasoning chain, the model samples many reasoning paths and selects the most consistent final answer across them. The self-consistency paper reported substantial gains on multiple reasoning benchmarks, including a large boost on GSM8K.

A complementary idea, scratchpad training, appeared in Nye et al.'s 2021 paper Show Your Work: Scratchpads for Intermediate Computation with Language Models. Instead of relying only on prompting at inference time, the model is trained on examples where intermediate computation is written out explicitly. The paper shows that transformers can learn to emit intermediate states for multi-step computation, effectively using the output sequence as a scratchpad. Akin to how forcing students to write out their work often improves performance, this method improved the models' performance.

Verification and Tool Use

The asymmetry between verification and generation, infamous in the complexity theory community (cue P = NP?), proved enormously useful. Cobbe et al. (2021) introduced a verifier-based setup in which many candidate solutions are generated and a trained verifier selects the best one. Their results showed that verification significantly improves performance on multi-step word problems. Process reward models (PRMs), developed further in Lightman et al.'s 2023 paper Let's Verify Step by Step, refine this idea by supervising and evaluating intermediate steps rather than only final answers. That paper reported that process supervision outperformed outcome supervision on MATH-style reasoning tasks.

Perhaps the most pragmatic advance was tool-augmented generation: allowing the model to write and run code instead of doing all arithmetic internally. OpenAI rolled out Code Interpreter to ChatGPT users in beta in July 2023. In this setup, the model can decompose a problem in natural language, invoke a deterministic computational tool such as Python, and then interpret the result. This sidesteps many weaknesses of pure token-based arithmetic and also generalises beyond arithmetic to symbolic and numerical computation.

Specialised Training

A related discovery was that training on technical material, including code and scientific text, improves mathematical reasoning. Minerva, released by Google Research in 2022, fine-tuned a large language model on technical content and achieved state-of-the-art performance on a range of quantitative reasoning benchmarks. The project showed that specialised training data can substantially improve mathematical and scientific problem solving even without external tools. Later open efforts such as WizardMath (2023) and MetaMath (2023) pushed further by using synthetic data generation, data augmentation, and filtered self-generated training data to improve mathematical reasoning in open models.

The release of GPT-4 in March 2023 marked another clear jump in capability. OpenAI's technical report documented strong performance across many academic and professional benchmarks, including AP Calculus BC. By the middle of 2023, the question had begun to shift from whether LLMs could do useful mathematics at all to how far the new techniques could carry them.

Test-Time Compute and Olympiad Territory

One of the most significant recent paradigm shifts was test-time compute scaling, exemplified by OpenAI's o1 family, first introduced in September 2024. OpenAI described o1 as a reasoning-oriented model trained to spend more time thinking before responding, using reinforcement learning to improve complex reasoning performance. Instead of expecting all reasoning ability to be compressed into a single fast pass, the system is allowed to allocate more inference-time computation to harder problems. OpenAI publicly highlighted strong gains on difficult benchmarks such as AIME and described older benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH as becoming less discriminative at the frontier. Akin to maths done by humans, hard problems are often solved not by instant insight but by trying approaches, checking cases, backtracking, and refining an argument.

At the frontier, DeepMind's AlphaGeometry and AlphaProof pushed mathematical AI into olympiad territory in 2024. AlphaGeometry was introduced in early 2024 as a system for Euclidean geometry that combines neural components with symbolic deduction, and DeepMind reported performance near the level of gold medalists on historical IMO geometry problems. Later that year, DeepMind announced AlphaProof, a reinforcement-learning-based system for formal mathematical reasoning, together with AlphaGeometry 2; the combined system solved four out of six IMO 2024 problems and reached silver-medal standard.

Towards Open Problems

Beyond solving known problems, LLM-based systems have also begun contributing to genuinely open ones. The first major example was DeepMind's FunSearch in late 2023: an evolutionary system that paired a code-generating LLM with an automated evaluator. Applied to the cap set problem, a long-standing challenge in extremal combinatorics, FunSearch discovered new constructions surpassing the best previously known results. In late 2025, Terence Tao and collaborators published a systematic study using DeepMind's AlphaEvolve, an LLM-powered evolutionary coding agent, on 67 problems spanning analysis, combinatorics, geometry, and number theory. AlphaEvolve rediscovered the best known results on most problems and improved the state of the art on several, including new constructions in combinatorial geometry. Two separate research papers by Tao followed directly from patterns that AlphaEvolve revealed but could not itself prove. As Tao noted, AlphaEvolve excels at discovering "constructions that were already within reach of current mathematics, but had not yet been discovered due to the amount of time and effort required." Perhaps the most vivid recent example came in early 2026, when Donald Knuth published a short paper titled "Claude's Cycles." Knuth had been working on a directed Hamiltonian cycle decomposition problem for a future volume of The Art of Computer Programming, with no general construction known. His colleague Filip Stappers fed the problem to Claude Opus 4.6, which, over 31 explorations in roughly one hour, tried and discarded several approaches before arriving at a working construction for all odd values of the parameter. Knuth then wrote the rigorous proof himself. He opened the paper with "Shock! Shock!" and wrote that he would "have to revise my opinions about generative AI." In all three cases, the pattern is the same: the LLM generates candidate constructions at a scale and speed that humans cannot match, but the verification, the proof, and the deeper understanding remain firmly human responsibilities.

These systems are best understood not as artificial mathematicians in the full human sense, but as increasingly powerful and still imperfect tools. LLMs can be excellent study aids, capable of generating examples, sketches, and alternative approaches, but they can also short-circuit the productive struggle that real mathematical learning requires. Note that the story is far from over: there are central open questions concerning reliability, formal integration, and whether these systems will eventually move from competent recombination to genuinely new mathematics.
