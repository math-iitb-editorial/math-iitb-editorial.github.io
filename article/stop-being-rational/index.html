Stop Being Rational

Author: Aditya Khambete
Published: 2026-04-03
"Freedom in a commons brings ruin to all."
Garrett Hardin, The Tragedy of the Commons

Adam Smith famously argued that individuals working in their own self-interest are guided by an "invisible hand" to promote the overall good of society. This is a beautiful idea philosophically, but mathematically, it is completely wrong.

Economists like to imagine a hypothetical species Homo economicus, a species of perfectly rational individuals who always make the most optimal decision for themselves. But if we pack a bunch of these individuals together, we do not get a utopian world—they actually create a gridlocked, crumbling dystopia.

In game theory, this gap between what is best for the individual and what is best for the group is measurable. Scientists call this the Price of Anarchy. Informally, it measures how much individual rationality harms the collective society.

Let us walk through this journey of selfishness and learn why rationality is stupid.

The Cost of Mistrust

This is one of the classic examples of how rationality backfires. Imagine two criminals get interrogated in separate rooms. They can either cooperate (C) by staying silent, or they can defect (D) by ratting the other out.

Player 1 Cooperate (C) $(2,\;2)$ $(5,\;1)$
Defect (D) $(1,\;5)$ $(4,\;4)$

Clearly, there are three possible outcomes:

  • Both cooperate: Neither of them confesses, so both get 2 years in prison each.
  • Both defect: They both rat each other out and get 4 years each.
  • One defects, one cooperates: The one who defects gets only 1 year, but the silent one gets thrown under the bus and receives 5 years.

Now let us see how a Homo economicus would solve this problem. Say Alice is sitting in her cell, thinking about what Bob would do:

  • If Bob defects: Alice stays silent and gets 5 years. If she rats him out, she gets 4 years. Defecting is clearly better.
  • If Bob cooperates: Alice cooperates and gets 2 years. If she rats him out, she gets only 1 year. Defecting is clearly better.

We can apply the exact same argument to Bob, and we realise that the optimal outcome under rational play is $(D, D)$, where both face 4 years in prison. Had they decided to be a little less rational and kept their mouths shut, they would have been out in 2 years.

This tragic optimal outcome is called the Nash Equilibrium: a position from which no player would want to change their action, given that no one else moves. For example, if Alice switches from D to C while Bob stays at D, Alice gets 5 years instead of 4. She has no incentive to change.

To measure how terrible this outcome is, we define the Price of Anarchy (PoA):

\[ \text{PoA} = \frac{\text{Cost of the worst Nash Equilibrium}}{\text{Cost of the social optimum}} \]

A natural question arises: how do we actually measure this social cost? A welfare function aggregates individual costs to calculate the societal cost. A natural choice is to sum all the individual costs; this is called the utilitarian welfare function. Another choice is to take the maximum of the individual costs; this is called the egalitarian welfare function. There are other choices (such as the product of costs), but we will not go into those. For the rest of this article, we will use the utilitarian welfare function.

Naturally, it is easy to see that $\text{PoA} \geq 1$. If $\text{PoA} = 1$, then the worst Nash Equilibrium is actually the social optimum—the "invisible hand" genuinely guides us to the optimal outcome. But if $\text{PoA} > 1$, selfishness actively harms society.

For instance, in the Prisoner's Dilemma, the social optimum is when both cooperate at $(2,2)$, giving a social welfare cost of $2 + 2 = 4$. The cost at equilibrium is $4 + 4 = 8$, so $\text{PoA} = 2$. We end up in a situation twice as bad due to selfishness.

A Failure to Build

Now imagine a society of $k$ individuals. They all live in suburbs ($s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_k$) and want to reach the city centre ($t$). They have two choices: either build their own private path, or chip in and build a multi-lane public highway.

  • Private path: Player $i$ can build a direct path at a cost of $1/i$, but this must be paid entirely by themselves.
  • Public path: Players can chip in to build a highway. Whoever contributes splits the bill of $1 + \epsilon$ equally.

We claim that everyone choosing their private path is a Nash Equilibrium. Anyone who moves unilaterally to the public path must bear the entire cost of $1 + \epsilon$, which is always more than the $1/k$ they would otherwise pay, for any positive $\epsilon$ and any $k \in \mathbb{N}$.

It is an easy exercise to verify that this is the only equilibrium. The total cost at equilibrium becomes $1 + \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{3} + \cdots + \tfrac{1}{k}$. This is just the $k$-th harmonic sum, so let us denote it by $H_k$. If we want to optimise for society's benefit, building only the public highway makes sense, which costs everyone an equal share of $1 + \epsilon$, giving a societal cost of precisely $1 + \epsilon$.

So we have:

\[ \text{PoA} = \frac{H_k}{1 + \epsilon} \]

It is a fundamental fact that $H_k$ diverges, and since $\epsilon$ is in our control, we can make the PoA arbitrarily large with an appropriate $k$. We have created a system where selfishness can lead to infinitely worse outcomes for society.

The Jam of the Damned

The previous section showed us that "rational" people would not let us build nice things. But now suppose the roads already exist. Imagine 1000 drivers want to go from point $A$ to point $B$. There are two paths:

  • Highway (top): Travel time worsens the more cars use it; $c(x) = x$, where $x$ is the fraction of users on this path.
  • The backroad (bottom): A bumpy dirt road that always takes exactly 1 hour, no matter how many people use it; $c(x) = 1$.

Every selfish driver wants the fastest route, so everyone ends up taking the highway until it is jammed and everyone needs 1 hour.

To calculate the social optimum, suppose a fraction $f$ uses the highway. The societal cost becomes:

\[ C = f \cdot f + (1 - f) \cdot 1 = 1 - f + f^2 \]

Taking the derivative and setting it to zero, we find $f = \tfrac{1}{2}$ is the optimal fraction, which minimises the social cost to $\tfrac{3}{4}$. The cost at equilibrium is 1. So we get:

\[ \text{PoA} = \frac{1}{3/4} = \frac{4}{3} \]

Even in this case, we have 33% extra inefficiency due to drivers being rational.

Braess's Paradox

We have seen enough to convince ourselves that selfish behaviour causes traffic. Surely the solution is to build more roads, right?

\[ C = \left(1 + \tfrac{1}{2}\right) \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} + \left(1 + \tfrac{1}{2}\right) \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} = \frac{3}{2} \]

Now suppose the city planner decides to be helpful and creates an ultra-fast, zero-cost bridge from point $A$ to point $B$:

A new shortcut opens up. For any single individual, taking the first half of the upper path and then using the bridge to the second half of the lower path is the best option. Since everyone reasons this way, everyone uses the shortcut, and the total cost becomes $1 + 1 = 2$—which is worse than our earlier cost of $\tfrac{3}{2}$.

Notice that we have added a new, magically fast road, and yet every person's commute has gotten worse.

Conclusion

The "invisible hand" that Adam Smith pointed out is deeply flawed when faced with shared resources among multiple agents. We often view centralised coordination as an abuse of authority. But game theory suggests otherwise. Sometimes restricting choices is not oppression—it is a mathematical necessity to save us from our own rationality.
