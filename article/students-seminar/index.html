The Students' Seminar

Interviewee: Aditya Dwivedi
Published: 2026-04-03

The Students' Seminar is an initiative launched by students of the Mathematics department at IIT Bombay once regular college life resumed after COVID-19. Brahadeesh Sankarnarayanan, a PhD alumnus, is to be credited with setting up the seminar and promoting student participation during the first two years. The first seminar was delivered by a B.S. Mathematics student, Madhur Agrawal, during the Fall semester of 2022. The seminar aims to encourage students to pick up a new topic in mathematics and deliver a 60-minute talk on it. This allows students to practice their presentation skills and learn how to deliver talks to a general, unspecialized mathematical audience. We present an interview with a current organiser of the Students' Seminar initiative, Aditya Dwivedi.

What motivated you to take up the role, and what are the duties that go into organising the seminar?

My main motivation for taking this job was that I liked the idea of seminars given by students, and I thought that this is an activity that ought to be better promoted in the department. Before taking on the organisational role, I had given two student seminars. I recall that my first seminar was horrible — I went with a vague title, and I did not manage to convey the content well, finishing in only thirty minutes. On the bright side, this experience gave me some ideas for presenting next time and showed me the pitfalls to avoid. I continued attending and watching others present, and I slowly improved. Since becoming the organiser, I have attended almost all the seminars (as is my obligation). I get to listen to topics I am not very comfortable with mathematically, which develops in me a fundamental questioning of Mathematics and lets me pick up new ways of presenting from the speakers. I think this practice needs to be promoted more in the department, at least among M.Sc. and B.S. students, as there is such a wide variety of fun Mathematics beyond coursework. We recently celebrated our 100th talk, and it has continued to go strong.

Organising is usually straightforward — we find a speaker, book a classroom, order snacks and send out an announcement email. I think budgeting was an issue back when it started post-COVID. Umesh Shankar brought reforms to how we order snacks, allowing us to have regular places outside campus where we can get our assortment. We hope to see the chocolate brownies return to the seminar menu soon!

Could you give us an idea about the demographics of the speakers and the attendees?

Most of the speakers to date have been PhD students. Besides them, we have had a few talks by B.S. students and one seminar by an M.Sc. Mathematics student so far. ⁠To quantify, I would say the B.S.-to-PhD ratio among speakers lies between 1:5 and 1:7. One potential cause of this skewed ratio towards PhD students, which has been cited by Masters students, is that Masters students are introduced to more advanced electives and reading courses after a good portion of their final year is done and this hampers their ability to participate more actively in the Students' Seminar. Another common misconception among B.S. and M.Sc. students is that the content of a talk must be highly non-trivial for it to be worth delivering. This could not be further from the truth — we have had many talks on seemingly simple topics, and these have allowed the speaker to focus on the many other aspects of stringing together a coherent talk, which goes a long way toward the career of any aspiring academic.

As for the attendees, the bias is even stronger towards the PhD students. Of course, another factor that affects the composition of the audience is the identity of the speaker (which naturally adds a few of the speaker's friends to the audience) and the content of the talk. Over the past year, I have been happy to see an increase in interest among B.S. and M.Sc. students in attending these talks. On a lighter note, I feel a budget restriction on snacks might have led to a drop in attendance.

How do you get people to participate in these seminars?

Towards the start of every semester, there are people who are very enthusiastic about giving seminars, with some usual suspects willing to fill these roles time and time again. At times, people use the Student's seminar to practice for another seminar/talk; this is especially common around the APS season for PhD students. For further talks, we usually ask around until we find a speaker, but it is always difficult to reach everyone uniformly. This year, we have tried our best to ensure a somewhat even split of topics.

What is the split of subjects in the Students' Seminar like? Are there more and less prevalent topics?

From the graduate students' side, the Partial Differential Equations and Commutative Algebra groups have been the most involved in the seminar in recent times, say, over the past two years. During the seminar's initial phase, we had many discussions on Combinatorics as well. Besides these subjects having a healthy number of graduate students, there might also have been an inherent bias due to the mathematical alignment of the seminar organisers, who have historically been associated with one of these three groups. Combinatorics is still very prevalent in the seminars, as undergraduates are introduced to it quite early in their degree, and in some cases even in high school. Furthermore, the subject lends itself to several interesting yet accessible talks.

I am pleased to report that we have had a more diverse set of topics than in previous years. Last year, I recall we had a month filled with only highly specialised Commutative Algebra seminars, which were hardly accessible to the vast majority. This year, on the other hand, we had talks on Algebra, Combinatorics, Probability, Partial Differential Equations, as well as some Operations Research topics, such as bandits and game theory. We hope to improve the diversity of topics in the future, but this comes with certain challenges. For example, niche areas like Ergodic theory and Dynamical systems will have very few speakers, although such topics would potentially be more appreciated by a larger audience due to their connection to Physics and other sciences. Statistics is one subject which we are trying to incorporate more of into the Students' Seminar, especially given the recent rise in the number of students in this field. We also hope to see more of Number theory and Topology in the years to come. Additionally, we are interested in having more of the so-called 'pop maths' talks, as they tend to be more popular with a general audience.

What do you think of the culture of research students in the department, and do you think this has helped improve it?

I would say that some students benefited a lot from these seminars, especially those in their first or second year of PhD. These students are regularly picking up new topics, and they frequently present results they like in the student seminar. The speakers who took these seminars more seriously used them as an opportunity to polish their presentation skills in other ways, such as preparing slides for their talks and distributing handouts.

However, it is quite unfortunate that the audience size has been dwindling over the past two years. In the early days of the seminar, we regularly had an audience of 10–15 students, but nowadays the number barely touches five. We look forward to seeing whether, in the new year, there will be a fresh enthusiasm for learning the craft of presenting.

The Students' Seminars will continue in the upcoming year, and any interested speakers can stay up to date by checking the official website.
