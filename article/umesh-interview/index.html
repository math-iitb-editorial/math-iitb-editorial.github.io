Life of a ΠhD — Adventure into Academia

Interviewee: Umesh Shankar
Published: 2026-04-07

Have you ever wondered what life as a PhD student in this department would be like? How is the research environment maintained, and what is the collaboration culture? Have you been curious about the potential paths to take after?

To shed light on some of these curiosities, we sat down with Umesh Shankar, a former PhD student in our department who did his PhD under the supervision of the current HoD Professor Krishnan Sivasubramanian. Here, he worked under the broad umbrella of combinatorics and related fields, with his thesis focusing on polynomials arising from permutation enumeration. These polynomials have strong analytic properties, such as being real-rooted. He developed methods to study these properties and worked on multiple conjectures, some of which still remain open. Before this, he obtained his BS-MS from IISER Trivandrum and through this interview he will shed light on his research journey.

Introduction to Mathematics

Initially, I did not like maths; in fact, I used to like history a lot more. When I was in second or third grade, my ambition was to be an archaeologist after watching a Jackie Chan animated series where he was one. I enjoyed reading about wars and kings, as well as the lives of peasants in medieval times. Later, I started taking chemistry and physics seriously and read about quantum mechanics, relativity, and black holes. Though I did not understand much, it all seemed like a lot of fun.

Maths was the least interesting subject for me because of how it was taught. Maths taught in school felt very much like the same question repeated over and over, with different coefficients. The lack of ingenuity made it seem uninteresting.

I later cleared the KVPY and joined IISER because I wanted to work at the interface of biology and chemistry, particularly in biochemistry. Later, I realised I did not enjoy laboratory work as much as theoretical work. Physics also lost my interest, so I was left with maths again. As a maths major and bio minor in my fifth year, I began looking at additive combinatorial problems for my thesis. Even then, I did not know if I wanted to do math full-time, but I did not like the idea of working without knowing why I was doing something. Math at least gives you that—you know why something is being done from start to finish.

Choosing IIT Bombay and Path to a PhD

After realising I wanted to do a PhD, I made many applications and attended interviews. I liked the questions asked during the IIT Bombay test and interview, and I thought they reflected well on the high research standards expected. This appealed to me greatly and led me to choose my institution. The biggest issue, though, in a PhD is finding the right guide. At IIT Bombay, sometimes you may not find anyone in your area because professors may already have students. I was fortunate that, at the time of my joining, the previous student had just graduated, so I was able to seamlessly take over as the next student. Though there is no fixed limit for the number of PhD students a professor can take, professors usually prefer to give personalised attention to students and opt to have at most one or two students at a time. Many are also limited by funding opportunities, which come from external organisations rather than the professor. Funding comes from the institute or from external sources such as GATE, NBHM, CSIR, or UGC-JRF. These organisations send stipends to IIT Bombay, which are then forwarded to students.

One thing I observed during my PhD preparation was that most students are comfortable with written exams but less so with interviews. Interviews involve real-time thinking and cross-questioning. Once an incorrect statement is made, it cannot be retracted. I may not get away with saying something as obviously wrong as rationals are not dense in real numbers in an interview, but in a written exam, I can notice my mistake and go back to fix it. A way to alleviate this nervousness is to practice discussing mathematics with peers. This discussion helps you learn how to explain concepts in an intelligible way. Performance anxiety is very real, and even strong candidates may not perform well in interviews.

Developing a Research Direction

My PhD journey was not linear. I started with problems left by my advisor's previous student. Some were solvable, others remained open. Later, I began exploring independently.

One key habit that I learnt from my advisor was to always remain up to date on recent papers put out on arXiv. This will allow you to understand the research landscape in a field and I took up this habit from him. I observed what problems were active and in demand and this led me to studying properties of polynomials, especially those arising from permutation enumeration.

Research Culture and Collaboration

IIT Bombay's mathematics department is well-balanced across different areas. The research culture is improving, especially in terms of discussion and collaboration. I have collaborated with multiple people—my advisor, Professor Niranjan, other PhD students, and postdocs. Collaboration makes research more social and less isolating. Struggling together reduces frustration.

Mathematics is becoming more collaborative globally due to better connectivity and access to resources. Earlier, research was more individual due to limited communication, but now collaboration is easier and more effective.

Thoughts on the Mathematics Research Landscape

Mathematical research usually starts with a single question, which leads to multiple new ones. Some are solved, while others remain open. A key realisation is that asking meaningful questions is a lot more important than solving them. Curiosity is essential. The ability to ask meaningful, interesting, and tractable questions defines a good researcher. Because of the abstract nature of mathematics, asking the right question becomes even more crucial because most of the time, there is no immediate real-world application to motivate a problem. Instead, the aesthetics of problems posed by a specific guide or field lend themselves to various research directions.

I am not so concrete about the field that I wish to stay in. Right now, it is questions in maths that I am finding interesting to ask, but it may be that I later find questions in various other fields, such as theoretical computer science, as problems of interest. Sometimes it does feel like theoretical computer science is more rewarding. Problems are more concrete and accessible earlier, whereas in mathematics, you often need to study for years before you can even ask a question. So it is more of an aesthetic of quick problems I am interested in answering now.

On top of this, publications take a long time in this field—sometimes 8–10 months just to find out whether a paper is accepted or rejected. Reviews can sometimes feel inadequate and like the person has not even read the paper, but that makes a passion for asking the right questions and finding solutions not being your only motivation even more crucial.

Undergraduate and Postgraduate Research Culture

Compared to other fields, mathematics research has a high barrier to entry. Students often spend years learning before they can even formulate new questions. This can feel unrewarding compared to fields like CS, where one can contribute earlier. Undergraduate research programs should focus on teaching students how to ask questions, not just solve existing ones. Programs like those at MIT or Caltech could serve as models for such an environment as they inculcate such curiosity in their students. Currently, many projects are reading-based rather than exploratory, which does not foster effective research thinking.

Many undergraduates lose interest in research despite initially having enthusiasm. This may be due to a lack of engagement, insufficient inspiration, or the demanding nature of the subject. Responsibility lies with PhDs, postdocs, and faculty to inspire students through teaching, discussion, and visible passion. More reading groups, problem-solving sessions, and open discussions are needed.

Final Remarks

I think the main thing a student needs to develop is the ability to ask many questions. This curiosity is what the department, along with its culture, should be promoting. There should also be more discussions, seminars, and visibility of ongoing work so that students can be inspired by the work of PhDs, postdocs, and faculty in the department, and so that research can be promoted more organically within the department.
