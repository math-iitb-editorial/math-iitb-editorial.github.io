An Undergraduate's Foray into Combinatorics

Author: Vishnu Ravi
Published: 2026-04-01

Hi, this is Vishnu Ravi. I am in my final year in the Mathematics department under the program B.S. Mathematics. A little bit about me, if I am not doing timepass in the maths department, you will find me yapping about Breaking Bad or visiting breakfast spots in Mumbai. I spent a lot of my time in IITB doing combinatorics, and thus naturally I'd wanted to talk about my experience with the subject.

My first introduction to combinatorics came naturally through math olympiads. I came across Enumerative Combinatorics by Stanley on my uncle's bookshelf (he was also a mathematician, though he sadly passed away before I was born). What immediately appealed to me about the book was that it had problems rated from 1 to 5, where 1 was very easy and 5 was an open problem. Being an ignorant 15-year-old, I skipped straight to a level-5 problem and sat with it for two weeks. Life moved on, and I eventually forgot about the problem — until I was asked to write this article. While the problem remained open, I had noticed that some researchers had managed to publish results since the time I had read it. In the meantime, I had begun my own experiments with combinatorics.

In my second year, I was naturally drawn back to combinatorics because of its seemingly elementary nature. By no means is the subject simplistic — at least not to me. Getting into combinatorics was somewhat like getting into IIT. It looks flowery from the outside, but once you are deep inside, it is a completely different beast.

I was drawn to combinatorics for two reasons:

Most combinatorial problems can be explained to anyone with a high-school understanding of mathematics. Since I enjoy discussing problems with people, this was a major plus.

I wanted to work on problems rather than just passively read theory, and combinatorics seemed the only avenue where I could do so meaningfully. (This, of course, I later found out to be untrue, with various other avenues being a possibility as well — but this was my naive second-year belief and also the reality of my second-year mathematical knowledge.)

I began by reading Enumerative Combinatorics by Stanley, working through the first two chapters. A natural next step was The Probabilistic Method by Alon and Spencer.

This was perhaps the first time I felt that mathematics was not merely useful, but genuinely beautiful — even aesthetically pleasing.

The probabilistic method can be stated quite simply: to prove the existence of a property in a discrete structure, one shows that under an appropriate random model, the probability that the property occurs is nonzero. The first time I encountered this idea, it felt almost "sus", but as I read further, I realised that at its core, the method is built on counting arguments and the pigeonhole principle.

In the first chapter of Alon and Spencer, I would often try to "reverse" the probabilistic method to obtain non-probabilistic proofs. This worked for the early examples. But in later chapters — where concentration inequalities or linearity of expectation played a crucial role — reversing the argument led to proofs that felt extremely unintuitive. My takeaway was that probabilistic tools open doors that would otherwise remain elusive. They allow us to see structure that the properties of probability reveal, which would otherwise be elusive.

As I began working more seriously on problems, I frequently encountered the work of great combinatorialists — Noga Alon being a prominent example. His papers would leave me both amazed and frustrated. The solutions were often startlingly creative, and I found myself astonished by the depth and ingenuity of the human mind. I immediately wanted to train myself to think in such ways. Little did I know how ambitious that goal was.

Over time, I realised that combinatorics is not merely about finding answers but about finding elegant ones. Different mathematicians have different notions of elegance, and they cultivate communities that appreciate their aesthetic. This, to me, is one of the most liberating aspects of mathematics: there is no single monopoly on beauty. Every student can discover it, define it personally, and yet share it in a way that connects them to others. I think to me this is fundamentally what describes the beauty of mathematics.
